title: Exercises
lede: Exercises for finding research problems, framing your work, and planning your PhD. Most take under an hour; several work well with a friend or your cohort.

<div class="exercise" markdown="1">
### Read a paper with the research rubric
<p class="meta">30 minutes · alone or in a group</p>

Pick two very different papers by faculty you might work with, ones that approach research and measure success differently. For each, write one sentence per question:

1. What is the problem or gap, and why is it interesting and important?
2. What are current approaches, and why are they not adequate?
3. What is new about this approach? Why is it impactful? Why will it be better? How does it work?
4. What did they find, why is it important, and what are the shortcomings and limitations?
5. How does it generalize? What are the new directions, implications, and next steps?

Then present one paper in three minutes, one slide per question. The goal is condensing the paper to its clearest, most compelling story.
</div>

<div class="exercise" markdown="1">
### Before → Then
<p class="meta">10 minutes · while listening to a talk or reading a paper</p>

Summarize a piece of work in two sentences: the state of the world *before* it, and the state of the world *then*, after it. If you can't, the speaker or author didn't make the contribution clear, or you missed it. Try it on your own project too.
</div>

<div class="exercise" markdown="1">
### Write your origin story
<p class="meta">30 minutes · alone</p>

Write a paragraph about what drives your interest in research: an experience, a question you couldn't stop thinking about, or a problem you saw up close. Use it to look past your current topic or method and consider problems and approaches you haven't thought of, and to find a project that combines your interests with your advisor's. It also becomes the core of a fellowship personal statement.
</div>

<div class="exercise" markdown="1">
### Map your research space
<p class="meta">45 minutes · works best at a whiteboard with someone else</p>

1. Write your problem in the middle. List the populations or contexts it affects and how you'd measure it.
2. **Problem abstraction:** What class of problems is this one instance of? List other places the same problem shows up.
3. **Active ingredient:** What do you hypothesize is making the difference?
4. **Current strategies:** How do people currently try to solve this class of problems, technical or not?
5. Draw lines between strategies, populations, and active ingredients. Circle the holes: combinations nobody has tried.

Example: a project on conversation branching for virtual patients led to the abstraction "where is retrying important?", which pointed to parallel work in sports, learning sciences, educational games, music, and design prototyping.
</div>

<div class="exercise" markdown="1">
### 10 research questions in 10 minutes
<p class="meta">10 minutes · alone, then share</p>

Set a timer and write ten research questions about your area, one per minute. Don't judge them as you go. Afterward, pick the two most promising and check each against this list: Can you articulate the hole in previous research, the impact if you fill it, the risks, your novel angle and methods, the scope and speed at which you can fill it, and how well it aligns with your interests and values?
</div>

<div class="exercise" markdown="1">
### Plan a lean test (MVP)
<p class="meta">30 minutes · alone</p>

Write down the core assumption your project depends on. Then design the smallest, cheapest thing you could do to validate or invalidate it: a handful of interviews, a paper prototype, a pilot study, or a quick look at existing data. List three pieces of evidence that this test will teach you what you need to know.
</div>

<div class="exercise" markdown="1">
### Explain it with common words
<p class="meta">15 minutes · alone</p>

Explain your research using only the thousand most common English words. Wherever you get stuck, you've found jargon you'll need to define for a general audience.
</div>

<div class="exercise" markdown="1">
### Record a two-minute talk
<p class="meta">1 hour · with a friend</p>

Prepare a two-minute talk on your research, record it, and watch it. Write down at least three things you notice about yourself and your slides. Then ask someone else what landed and whether you spoke too fast.
</div>

<div class="exercise" markdown="1">
### Advice for your future self
<p class="meta">5 minutes · after every class, talk, or meeting</p>

Write down what you learned as advice to your future self. Schedule an email to yourself for a date when you'll need it, such as the week before your first research talk.
</div>

<div class="exercise" markdown="1">
### Make a PhD curriculum
<p class="meta">1 hour · alone, then with your advisor</p>

List the experiences you want during your PhD: a project sequence, technical and research skills, reviewing papers, mentoring junior researchers, teaching, a doctoral consortium, a systematic literature review, giving talks, meeting faculty, and an outside hobby. Revisit it each year with your advisor. Start from this [PhD Curriculum (aka Bucket List) template](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1odv3E1QuMM6AUL3TuRaWxvKsZq8V90v27hpsOji1C6o/edit?usp=sharing).
</div>

<div class="exercise" markdown="1">
### Fill out the advising questionnaire
<p class="meta">30 minutes · alone, then with your advisor</p>

Make a copy of the [Advising Questionnaire](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1TgvBLfBLhpiI2MGhVXGeqbirrgCHgiFi0zyY6mnvXCE/edit?usp=drive_link), fill it out, and discuss it with your advisor at the start of your PhD to set expectations and boundaries.
</div>
