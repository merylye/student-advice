## Getting involved

- **Be proactive.** Don't take it personally if faculty/PhD students aren't approaching you. Initiate contact, come to office hours, and ask for specific things. It is much easier for a mentor to respond to a specific request than to a vague ask for mentorship.
- **Find multiple mentors.** Rather than searching for one perfect mentor, build a team where each person provides something you need. Mentors can be faculty, staff, or other students.

## Getting your first research position

- **Talk to professors directly.** Ask questions after class or in office hours, go to research talks, and ask to join labs whose work excites you. A few completed classes in the area make you a stronger candidate ([Hewitt](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~johnhew/undergrad-researchers.html); [Stanford CS](https://cs.stanford.edu/degrees/phd/PhD/GraduateSchoolAdvice.pdf); [Nakai and Guo](https://pg.ucsd.edu/publications/hidden-curriculum-undergrad-mentoring_ICER-2023.pdf); [CRA](https://conquer.cra.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/ThePhDinComputing_CRAEWebinar.pdf)).
- **Use courses as an entry point.** Take project-based and graduate-level courses, and consider turning a course project into research with that professor ([Stanford CS](https://cs.stanford.edu/degrees/phd/PhD/GraduateSchoolAdvice.pdf); [CRA](https://conquer.cra.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/ThePhDinComputing_CRAEWebinar.pdf)).
- **Apply for funded summer research.** Look into NSF REUs and your university's undergraduate research funding. Contact professors in late fall and by February, since deadlines cluster then ([Hewitt](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~johnhew/undergrad-researchers.html); [Stanford CS](https://cs.stanford.edu/degrees/phd/PhD/GraduateSchoolAdvice.pdf)).
- **Make time during the year.** Aim to work a few hours a week with a professor across a full academic year, and consider a lighter course load if you can still graduate on time ([Hewitt](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~johnhew/undergrad-researchers.html)).
- **Build a portfolio if you lack publications.** Projects, reimplementations, blog posts, and follow-up work on papers all count ([Krishnamurthi](https://parentheticallyspeaking.org/articles/us-cs-phd-faq/)). For computational social science, D-Lab's guide lists three steps: skills, a portfolio, and community ([D-Lab](https://dlab.berkeley.edu/news/introducing-three-step-guide-training-computational-social-science-students)).

## What research is like

- **Research uses different skills than coursework.** You choose the problems, they may have no known answer, and you may end up knowing more than anyone about your topic. Grades are a weak signal of research ability ([Hewitt](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~johnhew/undergrad-researchers.html); [CRA](https://conquer.cra.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/ThePhDinComputing_CRAEWebinar.pdf)). Most of your early attempts won't work. The skill you are building is figuring out what to try next.
- **Expect to spend time confused.** Not knowing what you don't know is part of starting out. Asking questions early saves weeks of going in the wrong direction.
- **Write to think and to learn.** Writing extends your reasoning, and writing down what you read helps you learn hard material ([Blum](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~mblum/research/pdf/grad.html)).

## The hidden curriculum

- **Expect unwritten rules.** Much of how research works, from authorship norms to how often to check in, is never written down, and not knowing these norms can feel like not belonging. First-generation students and others outside academic networks are often less comfortable asking ([Calarco](https://scatter.wordpress.com/2018/07/24/guest-post-grad-schools-hidden-curriculum/); [Nakai and Guo](https://pg.ucsd.edu/publications/hidden-curriculum-undergrad-mentoring_ICER-2023.pdf)).
- **Ask anyway.** Assume someone can teach you any new task, look for that information before struggling alone, and test whether professors will answer, since most will ([Calarco](https://scatter.wordpress.com/2018/07/24/guest-post-grad-schools-hidden-curriculum/)). A good first question for a senior student in the lab: what do you wish you had known when you joined?
- **Pass it on.** Share what you learn with newer students. Peer-written guides handed out in intro courses reach students who are not already well connected ([Calarco](https://scatter.wordpress.com/2018/07/24/guest-post-grad-schools-hidden-curriculum/); [Nakai and Guo](https://pg.ucsd.edu/publications/hidden-curriculum-undergrad-mentoring_ICER-2023.pdf)).

## Evaluating good research

A quick way to understand any paper, including your own project, is to answer five questions:

1. What is the problem or gap, and why is it interesting and important?
2. What are current approaches, and why are they not adequate?
3. What is new about this approach? Why is it impactful? Why will it be better? How does it work?
4. What did they find, why is it important, and what are the shortcomings and limitations?
5. How does it generalize? What are the new directions, implications, and next steps?

## Thinking about a PhD?

A PhD is a job where you are trained to become an independent researcher.

- **Try research before deciding.** Doing research is the best way to learn whether you like it, ideally alongside an internship or job in your field so you can compare ([Hewitt](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~johnhew/undergrad-researchers.html); [CRA](https://conquer.cra.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/ThePhDinComputing_CRAEWebinar.pdf)).
- **Talk to people about their days.** Ask professors, PhD students, and friends in industry what they like and dislike about their work ([Hewitt](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~johnhew/undergrad-researchers.html)).
- **Know your reason.** A PhD requires caring about a topic enough to generate your own questions, and a strong reason helps you persist through rejected papers and the mid-PhD slump. If you are unsure, working in industry first is fine ([Hewitt](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~johnhew/undergrad-researchers.html); [Krishnamurthi](https://parentheticallyspeaking.org/articles/us-cs-phd-faq/); [Blum](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~mblum/research/pdf/grad.html)).
- **Funding exists.** CS PhDs in the US are usually fully funded with tuition and a stipend. Plan for about six years ([Hewitt](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~johnhew/undergrad-researchers.html); [Krishnamurthi](https://parentheticallyspeaking.org/articles/us-cs-phd-faq/); [CRA](https://conquer.cra.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/ThePhDinComputing_CRAEWebinar.pdf)).
- **Master's first?** Stanford CS says an MS "isn't required!" and recommends applying straight to the PhD if you want research. Krishnamurthi suggests a research-track master's if your CS background is weak or you want to test research first, and CRA notes a master's may shorten the PhD ([Stanford CS](https://cs.stanford.edu/degrees/phd/PhD/GraduateSchoolAdvice.pdf); [Krishnamurthi](https://parentheticallyspeaking.org/articles/us-cs-phd-faq/); [CRA](https://conquer.cra.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/ThePhDinComputing_CRAEWebinar.pdf)).
- **Build toward three strong letters.** One professor who knows your work well is worth more than brief stints with many. Count your likely strong letters by junior year, and if you have fewer than three, work with more faculty ([Hewitt](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~johnhew/undergrad-researchers.html); [Stanford CS](https://cs.stanford.edu/degrees/phd/PhD/GraduateSchoolAdvice.pdf)).
