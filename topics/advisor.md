## Boss and mentor

Your advisor is your ***boss*** as well as your ***mentor***. As your boss, they pay for your tuition and stipend, so preferably, you do work that can be funded by a particular grant, in which you probably had little to no input. But hopefully, if you found the right advisor (which means aligning in terms of research interest AND personality), it shouldn't be too hard for you to figure out a project that fits both of your interests.

As your mentor, your advisor's purpose is to help you become an independent researcher. At some point, [the advisor-advisee dynamic will shift and you will go rogue](https://matt.might.net/articles/ways-to-fail-a-phd/), at which point you and your advisor might begin to diverge a bit. Hopefully not entirely, but just enough that sometimes you will have disagreements or different interests. Stick to your gut while also accepting their guidance. That's a balance you'll have to navigate.

## Set expectations early

At the start of your research journey, talk through expectations with your mentor: goals, hours, skills you want to learn, how you'll get feedback, and what success would look like for each of you.

- [Advising Questionnaire]({{ '/assets/files/advising-questionnaire.xlsx' | relative_url }}): I recommend making a copy and filling this out at the start of your PhD to set expectations and boundaries with your advisor.
- [Individual Development Plan (IDP) for Doctoral Students]({{ '/assets/files/individual-development-plan.docx' | relative_url }})
- [CRA mentor-mentee contract]({{ '/assets/files/mentor-mentee-contract.docx' | relative_url }})

More on expectations:

- **Ask what is expected of you each year.** Written expectations keep you from overworking toward impossible goals or underestimating the effort needed ([Vinitsky](http://eugenevinitsky.github.io/posts/expectation_setting.html)).
- **Raise trouble early.** If you're falling behind, talk with your advisor so you can find the cause together ([Vinitsky](http://eugenevinitsky.github.io/posts/expectation_setting.html)). If you're stuck for about a week, ask your advisor, a senior student, or a postdoc ([Guo](https://pg.ucsd.edu/early-stage-PhD-advice.htm)), and say "I'm overwhelmed" before a deadline turns into a crisis ([Marcus](https://marcua.net/writing/gradschool-guide/)).
- **Ask how much of each paper you should draft yourself** ([Guo](https://pg.ucsd.edu/early-stage-PhD-advice.htm)).

## Meetings and staying in touch

- **Treat meetings as help-seeking, not tests.** Presenting your week as positively as possible and hiding what you couldn't do leaves you struggling silently with the same problems. Bring the problems and questions you need help with, including soft skills like time management, losing motivation, or managing undergraduate RAs.
- **Your advisor wants you to succeed.** They plan to work with you for years. You are not being assessed for selection, as you were in undergrad; the goal is the shortest path to building you up as a researcher.
- **Bring decisions, not a status list.** Before the meeting, write down the decisions that need to be made to reach your next goal. Sort out which questions need your advisor, which a labmate could answer, and what specific actions you need from your advisor, such as a recommendation letter by a certain date.
- **Start with a recap.** Advisors are busy and often don't remember last week's meeting. Open by recapping what you decided last time, and keep running meeting notes for each project in a shared doc.
- **Keep in regular contact.** Advisors like students who stop by outside scheduled meetings ([APA, Dear Advisee](https://www.apa.org/monitor/2012/07-08/advisee)), and feedback every week or two reduces uncertainty and isolation ([Guo](https://pg.ucsd.edu/early-stage-PhD-advice.htm)). Send regular status reports to your advisor and committee ([Azuma](https://www.cs.unc.edu/~azuma/hitch4.html)).
- **Try first, then ask.** Solve what you can before bringing a problem to your advisor ([APA, Dear Advisee](https://www.apa.org/monitor/2012/07-08/advisee); [Azuma](https://www.cs.unc.edu/~azuma/hitch4.html)).
- **Bring specific options.** Ask your advisor to choose among a few concrete options, and come with prepared questions ([Guo](https://pg.ucsd.edu/early-stage-PhD-advice.htm)).
- **Follow up.** Busy faculty need reminders and aren't offended by them ([Guo](https://pg.ucsd.edu/early-stage-PhD-advice.htm)).
- **Ask for the feedback you need**, on theory and methods as well as writing ([APA, Dear Advisor](https://www.apa.org/monitor/2012/07-08/advisor)).

## Taking ownership

- **Drive your own projects.** Your advisor isn't a day-to-day boss, so create your own deadlines and accountability, such as lab talks and scheduled one-on-ones ([Guo](https://pg.ucsd.edu/early-stage-PhD-advice.htm)).
- **Contribute your own ideas.** Students want room to propose ideas beyond grunt work ([APA, Dear Advisor](https://www.apa.org/monitor/2012/07-08/advisor)). Chris Martens found that moving to a problem she devised herself was a turning point ([Martens](https://lambdamaphone.blogspot.com/2015/11/how-to-get-phd-in-computer-science-if.html)).
- **Make up for gaps.** No advisor is perfect, so take initiative where yours falls short ([Guo](https://pg.ucsd.edu/early-stage-PhD-advice.htm)).

## Methods and feedback

- Advisors steer students toward the methods they know best. If you want to learn other methods, build projects that use them and find other experts to help.
- Your advisor has much more experience using, publishing, and defending the methods they use often, so their feedback is most useful there.
- Read your advisor's papers and think about why they matter.

## The professional side

- **Keep it professional.** Leave personal matters mostly out of the relationship ([APA, Dear Advisee](https://www.apa.org/monitor/2012/07-08/advisee)), and as a funded RA, expect to deliver results ([Azuma](https://www.cs.unc.edu/~azuma/hitch4.html)).
- **Choose your committee carefully and listen to it.** Avoid members who are in conflict with your advisor ([APA, Dear Advisee](https://www.apa.org/monitor/2012/07-08/advisee)), and keep them informed, since they remember the advice they gave ([Might](https://matt.might.net/articles/ways-to-fail-a-phd/)).
- **Be good to staff and peers.** Department staff can help with pay, equipment, and paperwork ([APA, Dear Advisee](https://www.apa.org/monitor/2012/07-08/advisee)). Peers can give feedback on papers and tips on working with particular faculty ([Rexford](https://blog.citp.princeton.edu/2010/09/27/advice-new-graduate-students/)).

## Asking for recommendation letters

- **Ask people who know your research.** Research-based letters count much more than letters from course instructors ([Kamath](https://kamathematics.wordpress.com/2021/08/18/how-to-ask-for-a-letter-of-recommendation/); [Ko](https://faculty.washington.edu/ajko/faq)).
- **Give plenty of lead time.** Ko asks for at least two weeks and prefers a month; Kamath asks for at least six weeks ([Kamath](https://kamathematics.wordpress.com/2021/08/18/how-to-ask-for-a-letter-of-recommendation/); [Ko](https://faculty.washington.edu/ajko/faq)). Earlier is safer.
- **Ask whether they can write a *strong* letter.** This gives the writer a graceful way to decline ([Harchol-Balter](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~harchol/gradschooltalk.pdf); [Might](https://matt.might.net/articles/how-to-apply-and-get-in-to-graduate-school-in-science-mathematics-engineering-or-computer-science/)).
- **Make the writer's job easy.** Send a spreadsheet of programs and deadlines, your statement, CV, and transcript, and a list of concrete work you did with them. Give an early deadline, send reminders, thank them, and waive your right to see the letter ([Kamath](https://kamathematics.wordpress.com/2021/08/18/how-to-ask-for-a-letter-of-recommendation/); [Ernst](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~mernst/advice/academic-job.html); [Sap](https://maartensap.com/notes/01-apply-grad-school.html); [Sherry](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~justine/advice.pdf)).
- **Draft your own letter?** There's mixed advice.
  - [Ernst](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~mernst/advice/academic-job.html) suggests offering to help.
  - [Kamath](https://kamathematics.wordpress.com/2021/08/18/how-to-ask-for-a-letter-of-recommendation/) calls it unacceptable for writers to have applicants draft letters they sign, and advises being positive and specific if you're asked to.
