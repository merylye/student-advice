## Big P and little p

- **Big P** is the large problem in the state of the world. It should be worth caring about for everyone in your audience, and it frames why your work matters.
- **Little p** is the specific part of the big problem your work addresses. Research questions target a little p, and the gap usually sits at the little p level. Make the little p as specific as possible.
- A little p chips away at a facet of the big P, and there are little p's all the way down.
- Some studies, such as ethnographic work, surface new little p's instead of solving one. The results of any study can open new little p's.
- Keep the big P in view throughout a project; don't dig into little p's while losing sight of it.

## Novelty and gaps

- **Something nobody has studied is not automatically worth studying.** Nobody has tested whether you can sit in every chair, and it isn't a useful question. You don't need to study every chair to know a chair will hold your weight.
- **Don't exaggerate the distance from previous work.** It is very unlikely that nobody has looked into a question worth asking. Name the closest prior work on every dimension and say exactly what you do differently.
- **Let the justification fall out of the problem.** The gap should be a clear missing space or a consequential difference from previous approaches, not a contrived intersection.
- **Start from a real-world problem or puzzle**, then ask whether it also matters to academia. Strong questions matter to both.

## Formulating research questions

A good research question expresses a gap or new direction in the literature, is specific enough to be meaningful, can be answered with available methods and data, and provides the basis for understanding your paper's contribution. Expect to iterate between literature, data, and methods; it is normal to refine questions once you see what your data can answer.

- **Gap in the literature.** Existing knowledge is always incomplete: studies contradict each other, prior results are puzzling, some cases are understood while other important cases are not, or we know very little about a phenomenon. Your literature review should make the gap clear, and your research questions say exactly what you intend to fill in.
- **Meaningful in context.** Specificity depends on how mature the area is. A first paper on a new phenomenon can ask, in effect, "What's going on here?" In a mature area, questions fill in critical details or compare approaches in a crowded field.
- **Answerable.** This often requires iterating between literature, data, and analysis methods. It is not unusual to gain access to data and work backward to the questions it can answer.
- **A basis for your contribution.** Readers can only understand your contribution if they know what came before. Your research questions are a precise statement of what you intend to contribute.

For example, Gilbert and Karahalios (CHI 2009) asked: "The existing literature suggests seven dimensions of tie strength... As manifested in social media, can these dimensions predict tie strength? In what combination?" and "What are the limitations of a tie strength model based solely on social media?"

## Choosing a first project

Can you articulate:

- The hole in previous research
- The impact if you fill the hole
- The risks involved in trying to fill it
- The novel angle and methods you would use
- The scope and speed at which you can fill it
- How well it aligns with your interests and values

The Heilmeier Catechism, shared by Kathleen Carley, asks similar questions: What are you trying to do, with no jargon? How is it done today, and what are the limits of current practice? What is new in your approach, and why do you think it will succeed? Who cares? What are the risks? How long will it take? What are the midterm and final "exams" that check for success?

## Mapping a research space

- **Problem abstraction.** What class of problems is your problem one instance of?
- **Active ingredient.** What do you hypothesize is making the difference?
- **Current strategies.** How do people currently try to solve that class of problems, technical or not?

For example, a project on conversation branching for virtual patients (big P: not enough therapists) led to the abstraction "where is retrying important?", with parallel spaces in sports, learning sciences, educational games, music, and design prototyping. Those analogies pointed to new research questions. See the exercise on [Try this](../../try-this/).

## Reading and searching the literature

- **Ask an expert.** Find people who have already synthesized the literature for you, from the top down (review papers, book chapters) and from the bottom up (one strong paper, expanded outward). Your advisor's papers and what they cite, related work sections, course reading lists, labmates, and the authors themselves are all good starting points.
- **Turn each search into a question.** Break your area into smaller p's or subquestions first. Each search should be a question, not a cluster of broad ideas.
- **Track your keywords.** Start from your research question, circle the most important terms, think of synonyms, and collect new keywords from your first pass. Ask scientists in nearby areas what they call it.
- **Follow citations both ways**, and expand from a few strong papers instead of constantly starting new threads.
- **Keep a reading list that supports synthesis**, not only a pile of PDFs. Write down each paper's key contribution to you, and share the list (with your keywords) with your advisor so they can spot blind spots.
- **Don't use LLMs to find references.** They make up literature, favor work that is popular in the press, and misattribute credit. They can help summarize papers, label content, or get past writer's block, but check everything.

## Moving fast: lean research

Identify your core assumption, then design the smallest, cheapest test that would validate or invalidate it: a few interviews, a lightweight prototype, a pilot study, or a quick scrape of existing data. Before you start, write down three pieces of evidence that this test will teach you what you need to know.
