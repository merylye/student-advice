## Structuring a paper

- *My 2 cents on formatting a paper:* Pretend like people are only going to read the headers and look at the pretty pictures.
- **Study papers you admire.** Pick a few best papers of the type you're writing and annotate what each section and paragraph is trying to accomplish. You don't need to read them in detail to learn their structure.
- **Use an outline.** Jacob Wobbrock's outline for an HCI paper is a good default (see resources below).
- **Explain your work in plain words first.** If you can explain it using only the most common words, you understand it well enough to write it.

## Literature reviews and selling your ideas

From Kathleen Carley's practicum talk:

- **A literature review is an argument.** It is key to "selling" your research. Summarize what has been done, what has not been done, and what needs to be done, and link any research question or hypothesis clearly to the review.
- **Don't try to cover everything.** Select the research most relevant to your paper and use those studies as evidence that your research is important and your findings relevant.
- **Literature appears throughout a paper.** In the introduction it establishes potential impact; in data and methods it credits others and offers comparisons; in the discussion it shows how your work confirms or extends others'.
- **Do four things with the literature:** summarize, synthesize, compare, and critique. By the end, you should be able to say what we know, what we don't know, what needs to be done, and how your research contributes.
- **Write a core statement.** One or two sentences that describe the area, its connection to improving something, and the open question. Make it straightforward, concrete, and testable.
- **Avoid common errors:** a review that isn't logically organized, doesn't relate the literature to your study, leaves out a major author or paper, reads like a series of disjointed summaries, or doesn't argue a point.

## Becoming a writer

- Write the parts you can, early. Drafts are easier to fix than blank pages.
- Ask for feedback before you think the draft is ready.
- Consider writing a public blog post about each paper; it forces you to explain why the work matters.
