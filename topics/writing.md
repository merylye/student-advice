## The parts of an HCI paper

Jacob Wobbrock's [annotated CHI paper template](https://faculty.washington.edu/wobbrock/pubs/Wobbrock-2015.pdf) explains what each section should do:

- **Title.** Engaging without being cute.
- **Abstract.** Describe the work, not the paper. Use at most one sentence of motivation, then what you did and what you found, with specific numbers, in one paragraph under 200 words.
- **Introduction.** Five moves: the state of the world, the big "BUT" (a problem or opportunity), what you did, your key findings, and your one to three contributions. A gap in the literature is weak motivation on its own, and "more research is needed" should not be your main implication. "Over-claiming is a sure way to get your paper rejected."
- **Related work.** Who did relevant work, what they found, and how your work differs. Group prior work by theme so it teaches the reader, and differentiate on question, method, technology, or result instead of claiming superiority.
- **Design of the thing you built.** A replicable account of the system's goals, principles, tradeoffs, design process, and limitations, with figures placed right after the text that refers to them.
- **Method.** Enough detail to meet your field's norms. For lab experiments: participants, apparatus, procedure, and design and analysis. For qualitative field studies: participants (with thick description and how you gained access), theory, procedures, and analysis.
- **Results.** Report findings in an organized way and save interpretation for the discussion. Organize lab results by dependent variable and field results by emergent theme, and "tell a story with data."
- **Discussion.** What the results mean, what surprised you, whether you met the introduction's promises, and what did or did not work, with an optional limitations paragraph.
- **Future work and conclusion.** One or two big-picture next steps instead of a to-do list, then confirm you delivered what the introduction claimed.

## Structuring the paper

- **Outline before you draft.** An outline is cheaper to change than paragraphs and shows you the flow ([Das](https://medium.com/gt-ups-course/resources-for-academic-writing-in-hci-82a27249cb5f); [Durand](http://people.csail.mit.edu/fredo/PUBLI/writing.pdf); [Vetterli](http://mri.beckman.illinois.edu/resources/good_paper.pdf)). Plan your figures at the outline stage too ([Durand](http://people.csail.mit.edu/fredo/PUBLI/writing.pdf)).
- **Titles: catchy or informative?** Wobbrock and Vetterli accept a catchy title as long as it isn't cute. Durand prefers informative titles, after a quotable but vague title sent one of his papers to the wrong reviewers.

## Telling the story

- **Find one main theme.** If you can't summarize the paper in one simple sentence, the story isn't clear yet ([Clemens, LSE Impact Blog](https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/impactofsocialsciences/2018/05/21/writing-a-page-turner-how-to-tell-a-story-in-your-scientific-paper/); [Durand](http://people.csail.mit.edu/fredo/PUBLI/writing.pdf)).
- **State the problem precisely.** Set up a clear gap or a method that fails ([Clemens](https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/impactofsocialsciences/2018/05/21/writing-a-page-turner-how-to-tell-a-story-in-your-scientific-paper/)).
- **Order by logic, not chronology.** Don't narrate the project in the order you did it ([Clemens](https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/impactofsocialsciences/2018/05/21/writing-a-page-turner-how-to-tell-a-story-in-your-scientific-paper/); [Vetterli](http://mri.beckman.illinois.edu/resources/good_paper.pdf)).
- **Motivate each component.** Introduce each piece through the challenge it solves, and explain why you made each choice ([Durand](http://people.csail.mit.edu/fredo/PUBLI/writing.pdf)). Results should "make a point" ([Vetterli](http://mri.beckman.illinois.edu/resources/good_paper.pdf)).
- **Report results plainly.** A p = 0.43 is not "trending towards significance" ([Das](https://medium.com/gt-ups-course/resources-for-academic-writing-in-hci-82a27249cb5f)).
- **Give the discussion room.** Discussions are often too short ([Clemens](https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/impactofsocialsciences/2018/05/21/writing-a-page-turner-how-to-tell-a-story-in-your-scientific-paper/)).

## Clarity for the reader

- **Assume the reader knows little.** Guide them step by step, and favor clarity over elegant prose ([Durand](http://people.csail.mit.edu/fredo/PUBLI/writing.pdf); [Vetterli](http://mri.beckman.illinois.edu/resources/good_paper.pdf)).
- **Use short, plain sentences**, one idea per sentence ([Durand](http://people.csail.mit.edu/fredo/PUBLI/writing.pdf); [Vetterli](http://mri.beckman.illinois.edu/resources/good_paper.pdf); [Das](https://sauvik-das.medium.com/the-day-you-became-an-academic-writer-2706ae01b2d1)).
- **Preview, then deliver.** Tell readers what you will tell them, and open each section with its key points ([Bertsekas](http://www.mit.edu/~dimitrib/Ten_Rules.html); [Durand](http://people.csail.mit.edu/fredo/PUBLI/writing.pdf)).
- **Repetition: yes or no?** Durand says to repeat important points. Vetterli says "Repeat NO, develop YES."
- **Keep terms and notation consistent.** Durand suggests writing a private glossary to force precise definitions ([Bertsekas](http://www.mit.edu/~dimitrib/Ten_Rules.html); [Durand](http://people.csail.mit.edu/fredo/PUBLI/writing.pdf)).
- **Make figures self-contained.** Captions should make sense without the text, and figures should appear near where they're cited ([Durand](http://people.csail.mit.edu/fredo/PUBLI/writing.pdf); [Vetterli](http://mri.beckman.illinois.edu/resources/good_paper.pdf)).
- **Cut words.** Write "we found" instead of "in this paper, we have found," and drop "Note that" ([Durand](http://people.csail.mit.edu/fredo/PUBLI/writing.pdf); [Vetterli](http://mri.beckman.illinois.edu/resources/good_paper.pdf)).

## Literature reviews and selling your ideas

- **A literature review is an argument.** It is key to "selling" your research. Summarize what has been done, what has not been done, and what needs to be done, and link any research question or hypothesis clearly to the review.
- **Don't try to cover everything.** Select the research most relevant to your paper and use it as evidence that your research is important and your findings relevant.
- **Literature appears throughout a paper.** In the introduction it establishes potential impact; in data and methods it credits others and offers comparisons; in the discussion it shows how your work confirms or extends others'.
- **Do four things with the literature:** summarize, synthesize, compare, and critique. By the end, you should be able to say what we know, what we don't know, what needs to be done, and how your research contributes.
- **Write a core statement.** One or two sentences that describe the area, its connection to improving something, and the open question.
- **Avoid common errors:** a review that isn't logically organized, doesn't relate the literature to your study, leaves out a major author or paper, reads like a series of disjointed summaries, or doesn't argue a point.

## The writing process

- **Write regularly.** Write a little every day, and read well-written work ([Das](https://medium.com/gt-ups-course/resources-for-academic-writing-in-hci-82a27249cb5f); [Vetterli](http://mri.beckman.illinois.edu/resources/good_paper.pdf)).
- **Write early in the project.** Early writing exposes flaws in your thinking while you can still change course ([Das](https://medium.com/gt-ups-course/resources-for-academic-writing-in-hci-82a27249cb5f); [Durand](http://people.csail.mit.edu/fredo/PUBLI/writing.pdf)).
- **Where to start?** Durand starts with the overview. Das outlines the introduction first but writes it last, and starts with the methods when stuck.
- **Expect to rewrite.** Delete and redo sections, and let drafts sit before rereading ([Durand](http://people.csail.mit.edu/fredo/PUBLI/writing.pdf); [Vetterli](http://mri.beckman.illinois.edu/resources/good_paper.pdf)).
- **Get outside readers.** Ask non-authors to read drafts, and read your work aloud. Early feedback should target scope and approach; later feedback should target exposition ([Durand](http://people.csail.mit.edu/fredo/PUBLI/writing.pdf)).

## Sharing your work publicly

- **Blog each paper.** A short post lets you tell your own story and reaches busy colleagues and the communities your work affects ([Fiesler](https://cfiesler.medium.com/why-and-how-academics-should-blog-their-papers-e6869559b8ea)).
- **Write the post for outsiders.** No jargon or acronyms, skip the methods, link the paper, keep it near 800 words, and add an image. It takes a couple of hours ([Fiesler](https://cfiesler.medium.com/why-and-how-academics-should-blog-their-papers-e6869559b8ea)).
- **Share code and data** with documentation so others can reproduce your work ([Vetterli](http://mri.beckman.illinois.edu/resources/good_paper.pdf)).
