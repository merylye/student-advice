## Structuring a talk

- **A talk is an advertisement for the paper.** A short talk should make listeners want to read the paper, so give context, motivation, and intuition instead of full detail ([Shewchuk](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~jrs/speaking.html); [Ernst](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~mernst/advice/giving-talk.html)).
- **Decide the takeaway first.** Name the one idea or few key points the audience should leave with, and cut everything that doesn't support them ([Shewchuk](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~jrs/speaking.html); [Ernst](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~mernst/advice/giving-talk.html)).
- **Answer three questions:** why the problem matters, what's new about your approach, and what evidence shows it works ([Shewchuk](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~jrs/speaking.html); [Ernst](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~mernst/advice/giving-talk.html)).
- **Fit the talk to the audience.** A research-group talk, a conference talk, and an invited talk have different goals ([Ernst](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~mernst/advice/giving-talk.html)).
- **Motivate the question for a diverse audience.** Start with the big P to place the topic in a relatable context, then zoom in to the little p.
- **Set up your central message early.** When it arrives late, it seems disconnected from everything before it.
- **Be explicit about your novelty** and what past work misses.
- **In a short talk, less is more.** Focus on the key takeaway and one research question. Covering everything makes the audience miss what matters.
- **Points connected in your head may not be connected for the audience.** Make links between examples, prior work, and your main point explicit, and bridge transitions between parts of the talk.
- **One case study can beat several examples** whose comparison the audience never sees.
- **Cut what you don't have time to explain** instead of using shorthand for the sake of completeness.
- **Define jargon** and use concrete examples for abstract ideas.
- **End with the new little p's your work creates** and why they matter.
- **Anticipate questions** from people you haven't met, and build a model of how others think.

## Designing slides

- **Make each slide title state the slide's point.** Informative titles help listeners who drift off, and test whether you understand each slide ([Shewchuk](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~jrs/speaking.html); [Ernst](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~mernst/advice/giving-talk.html)).
- **Use few words and large type.** Keep text minimal, use large sans-serif fonts, and prefer real figures over text and clip art ([Shewchuk](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~jrs/speaking.html); [Ernst](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~mernst/advice/giving-talk.html)).
- **Remove clutter:** logos, repeated conference names, decorations, and transitions ([Shewchuk](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~jrs/speaking.html); [Ernst](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~mernst/advice/giving-talk.html)).
- **Don't read your slides.** Explain what matters and how the parts connect ([Shewchuk](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~jrs/speaking.html); [Ernst](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~mernst/advice/giving-talk.html)).
- **Outline slides?** Ernst says never open with one and use one only at major section breaks; Shewchuk recommends brief section slides for talks of 30 minutes or more.
- **How to end?** Shewchuk ends by saying "Thank you" to cue applause. Ernst advises against a "Thank you" or "Questions" slide and leaves a contributions slide on screen during Q&A. You can do both: say thank you while showing your conclusions.

## Delivery

Things people notice when they first watch a recording of themselves:

- More filler words than expected: "um," "uh," "so," "basically," and phrases like "One thing that's interesting is..." used to buy time.
- Gestures that don't mean anything, rocking, and one overused emphasis gesture.
- Looking at the screen or projector instead of the audience.
- Speaking too fast, or at one constant pace. Pause longer than feels natural for emphasis. Varying your tone may feel fake at first.
- A weak opening. How you walk on and say your first words sets your credibility.
- Long, hedged answers in Q&A. Drop "I guess" and answer the question.
- Too much text on slides. Use highlighting and opacity to direct attention.
- Projector colors that look very different from your laptop.

From the talk guides:

- **Give practice talks to real listeners.** Shewchuk suggests three; Ernst suggests small audiences that mix trusted and skeptical listeners ([Shewchuk](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~jrs/speaking.html); [Ernst](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~mernst/advice/giving-talk.html)).
- **Use pauses.** Allow silence instead of "um," and if you get flustered, take a drink and continue. Don't announce that you're running out of time ([Shewchuk](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~jrs/speaking.html); [Ernst](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~mernst/advice/giving-talk.html)).
- **Repeat each question before answering it**, and say "I don't know" when you don't ([Ernst](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~mernst/advice/giving-talk.html)).

Record yourself at least once. It's uncomfortable and very useful. Because your own content is so familiar, ask others what landed and whether you spoke too fast.

## Elevator pitches

- **Prepare a 30- to 60-second version of your research.** Focus on your goals and why the work excites you, and leave out technical details ([Academia Stack Exchange](https://academia.stackexchange.com/questions/1779/what-are-the-elements-of-an-effective-elevator-pitch); [Vaughan](https://medium.com/@jennwv/nine-things-i-wish-i-had-known-the-first-time-i-came-to-nips-b939330661ed)). Try different hooks and frames, and aim to create a knowledge gap that makes the listener ask a question.
- **Make versions for different audiences:** experts in your subfield, your broader field, and the general public. Rehearse until it comes without effort ([Academia Stack Exchange](https://academia.stackexchange.com/questions/1779/what-are-the-elements-of-an-effective-elevator-pitch); [Vaughan](https://medium.com/@jennwv/nine-things-i-wish-i-had-known-the-first-time-i-came-to-nips-b939330661ed)).
- **Connect your pitch to the other person's.** When someone tells you their work, start yours with a specific connection ("I also look at how people learn, but in the context of interface design"), not "I also do HCI research."

## Conferences and networking

- **Talk to people.** Research involves much more socializing than most of us anticipated.
- **Skip some talks to make room for conversations.** Much of a conference's value is in hallway conversations, meals, and dinners, and overscheduling leads to burnout ([Vaughan](https://medium.com/@jennwv/nine-things-i-wish-i-had-known-the-first-time-i-came-to-nips-b939330661ed); [Cai](https://medium.com/syncedreview/neurips-2018-through-the-eyes-of-first-timers-5156384900bd)).
- **Use questions as openers.** Ask speakers questions after their talks, ask basic questions at posters, and talk to strangers at lunch ([Vaughan](https://medium.com/@jennwv/nine-things-i-wish-i-had-known-the-first-time-i-came-to-nips-b939330661ed); [Cai](https://medium.com/syncedreview/neurips-2018-through-the-eyes-of-first-timers-5156384900bd)).
- **Expect to feel out of place.** Many attendees are new too, and impostor feelings are common ([Vaughan](https://medium.com/@jennwv/nine-things-i-wish-i-had-known-the-first-time-i-came-to-nips-b939330661ed); [Cai](https://medium.com/syncedreview/neurips-2018-through-the-eyes-of-first-timers-5156384900bd)).
- **Prioritize peers.** Your most lasting connections are often with people at your own career stage, and accepting their invitations introduces you to others ([Vaughan](https://medium.com/@jennwv/nine-things-i-wish-i-had-known-the-first-time-i-came-to-nips-b939330661ed)).
- **Prepare and follow up.** Before the event, pick people to meet and read their work. Within a few days afterward, email new contacts and mention specific ideas you discussed ([International Conference Alerts](https://internationalconferencealerts.com/blog/how-to-make-a-plan-to-attend-international-academic-conferences/); [Cai](https://medium.com/syncedreview/neurips-2018-through-the-eyes-of-first-timers-5156384900bd)).
- **Take breaks**, especially if you're an introvert ([Vaughan](https://medium.com/@jennwv/nine-things-i-wish-i-had-known-the-first-time-i-came-to-nips-b939330661ed)).
- **Explore outside your area.** Attend a tutorial (a crash course for non-experts) or a workshop on an unfamiliar topic ([Vaughan](https://medium.com/@jennwv/nine-things-i-wish-i-had-known-the-first-time-i-came-to-nips-b939330661ed); [Cai](https://medium.com/syncedreview/neurips-2018-through-the-eyes-of-first-timers-5156384900bd)).
- **Socialize outside your research group**, find mentors, and become one.

## Visibility and organizing events

- **Communicate often and through several channels.** Blogs, social media, and videos add to papers and get you faster feedback ([Das](https://sauvik-das.medium.com/always-be-epic-97bf5dcd8ba5)).
- **Organizing a workshop takes months and a team.** Start about seven months ahead with at least two co-organizers, recruit speakers before the proposal deadline, keep backup reviewers and speakers, and start fundraising as soon as you're accepted ([Eysenbach and Bhupatiraju](https://medium.com/@erl.leads/hitchhikers-guide-to-organizing-an-academic-workshop-cc9a5b1c32c9)).
