## Structuring a talk

- **Motivate the question for a diverse audience.** Start with the big P to place the topic in a relatable context, then zoom in to the little p.
- **Set up your central message early.** When it arrives late, it seems disconnected from everything before it.
- **Be explicit about your novelty** and what past work misses.
- **In a short talk, less is more.** Focus on the key takeaway and one research question. Covering everything makes the audience miss what matters.
- **Points connected in your head may not be connected for the audience.** Make links between examples, prior work, and your main point explicit, and bridge transitions between parts of the talk.
- **One case study can beat several examples** whose comparison the audience never sees.
- **Cut what you don't have time to explain** instead of using shorthand for the sake of completeness.
- **Define jargon** and use concrete examples for abstract ideas.
- **End with the new little p's your work creates** and why they matter.
- **Anticipate questions** from people you haven't met, and build a model of how others think.

## Delivery

Things people notice when they first watch a recording of themselves:

- More filler words than expected: "um," "uh," "so," "basically," and phrases like "One thing that's interesting is..." used to buy time.
- Gestures that don't mean anything, rocking, and one overused emphasis gesture.
- Looking at the screen or projector instead of the audience.
- Speaking too fast, or at one constant pace. Pause longer than feels natural for emphasis. Varying your tone may feel fake at first.
- A weak opening. How you walk on and say your first words sets your credibility.
- Long, hedged answers in Q&A. Drop "I guess" and answer the question.
- Too much text on slides. Use highlighting and opacity to direct attention.
- Projector colors that look very different from your laptop.

Record yourself at least once. It's uncomfortable and very useful. Because your own content is so familiar, ask others what landed and whether you spoke too fast.

## Elevator pitches

- **Prepare a 30-second version of your research.** Try different hooks and frames, and aim to create a knowledge gap that makes the listener ask a question.
- **Connect your pitch to the other person's.** When someone tells you their work, start yours with a specific connection ("I also look at how people learn, but in the context of interface design"), not "I also do HCI research."

## Conferences and networking

- Talk to people. Research involves much more socializing than most of us anticipated.
- Socialize outside your research group, find mentors, and become one.
- Volunteering and helping organize workshops are good ways to meet people early in the PhD.
